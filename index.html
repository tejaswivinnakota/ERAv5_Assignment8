Attention was never wrong.
It was just expensive.

Every mechanism on this page is somebody looking at that bill and trying to pay less of it. They are laid out in the order they actually appeared — not the order they get taught, and not grouped by family — because the order is the argument.

In 2017 attention bought you something remarkable: every token could look at every other token, exactly, in one step. The cost was quadratic compute and a key-value cache that grows without limit. Everything since is a negotiation over that invoice.

Read the timeline top to bottom and you can watch the field change its mind. First it wants exactness. Then it wants memory back. Then it wants length. Then, having thrown memory away to get length, it goes back and builds memory again — on purpose this time. You cannot see that in a list. You can only see it on a timeline, and once you see it you can make a decent guess at what comes next.

The one you have to understand first

Nothing after 2017 makes sense without this, so it is worth being precise about what the original mechanism actually does — and exactly where the money goes.

Scaled dot-product attention, in one breath

Every token emits three vectors: a query (what am I looking for), a key (what am I), and a value (what I will hand over if you pick me). Score every query against every key with a dot product, divide by √dk so the numbers stay in a range softmax can handle, softmax the row into a probability distribution, and take that weighted average of the values.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dₖ ) V

That is the whole idea. It is content-addressed lookup, done softly and differentiably, with no recurrence and nothing to unroll — which is precisely why it trains in parallel and why it took over.

And here is the invoice

Three separate costs, and they are worth keeping apart, because different mechanisms attack different ones:

1. Training compute
O(n²·d)
Double the context, quadruple the work. This is what sparsity and linear attention attack.
2. Inference memory & bandwidth
KV cache = 2 · L · H · dh per token
Read in full for every single token generated. This is what MQA, GQA and MLA attack, and at generation time it dominates.
3. Length generalisation
breaks past training length
Not a cost in FLOPs but in capability. This is what the positional-encoding family attacks.

One distinction the rest of the page depends on

The quadratic term and the KV cache are different problems and they peak at different times. Quadratic compute hurts during training and during the prefill of a long prompt. The KV cache hurts during decoding, one token at a time, where you are not compute-bound at all but memory-bandwidth-bound. A mechanism that halves your FLOPs and leaves the cache alone will not make your chatbot feel faster. This is the single most common way people mis-pick a mechanism, and it is why the timeline below has two distinct threads running through it rather than one.

The bill nobody mentions until they deploy

Attention's famous cost is the quadratic one. The cost that actually decides whether you can serve a model is the KV cache — and unlike FLOPs, it is read in full for every token you generate. Set a model and a context length and watch why MQA, GQA and MLA were invented.

Lab 03

Where does the model learn where it is?

Attention itself is completely order-blind — shuffle the tokens and the maths is unchanged. Every positional scheme is a different answer to that, and the context-extension family is a long argument about what to do when you need a position the model has never seen.

Lab 04

Put any three side by side

The most useful thing you can say about these mechanisms is not which is best. It is that a mechanism which is right for a 2K chatbot can be flatly wrong for a 1M-token agent, and the reverse, and that neither of those facts makes it a bad mechanism. Pick three and read across.
